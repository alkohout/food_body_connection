Model Metrics Guide

Understanding Model Performance

This guide explains how to interpret the performance of the allergen ranking model. The model analyses your logged allergen and symptom data to highlight potential patterns. It is a tool for guidance, not a medical diagnosis.

Metrics and Interpretation

  • ROC AUC: measures the model's ability to distinguish likely from unlikely allergens.
    ● ≥ 0.75: Good – strong separation
    ● 0.65 – 0.74: Moderate – some useful signal
    ● < 0.65: Weak – limited reliability

  • Symptom Recall: the proportion of symptom-linked allergens the model correctly identifies.
    ● ≥ 0.70: Good – most triggers captured
    ● 0.55 – 0.69: Moderate – some triggers may be missed
    ● < 0.55: Low – use results with caution

  • Sample Size: the number of logged allergen–symptom events used by the model.
    ● ≥ 200: Good – reliable patterns
    ● 75 – 199: Moderate – patterns emerging
    ● < 75: Low – preliminary, results may be unstable

Dose–Response (GAM) Metrics

These metrics apply to the dose–response curves, which model how symptom burden changes with increasing exposure (e.g. volume consumed). The curves are estimated using a Generalised Additive Model (GAM), which allows for flexible, non-linear relationships.

  • Pseudo R²: measures how much of the variation in symptom burden is explained by the dose–response curve.
    ● ≥ 0.30: Strong signal – dose explains symptoms well
    ● 0.15 – 0.29: Moderate signal – partial relationship
    ● < 0.15: Weak signal – little explanatory power

  • Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF): reflects how complex or “wiggly” the curve is.
    ● ≤ 5: Smooth, stable relationship
    ● 6 – 8: Moderately complex – interpret carefully
    ● > 8: Highly complex – possible overfitting

  • Sample Size: the number of dose–symptom observations used to fit the curve.
    ● ≥ 40: Reliable curve estimation
    ● 15 – 39: Emerging pattern
    ● < 15: Insufficient data – curve may be unstable

Note: Dose–response curves assume that logged volume reflects meaningful exposure and that symptom burden combines intensity and frequency consistently. If logging is sparse or inconsistent, confidence will be lower even if a curve is shown.

Risk & Statistical Evidence

These metrics describe how symptom risk differs between days with and without exposure to a specific allergen, and how confident we are that the difference is not due to chance.

  • Absolute Risk Increase: the change in the chance of having symptoms on exposure days compared with non-exposure days.
    A positive value means symptoms were more common on exposure days.
    A negative value means symptoms were less common on exposure days.

  • p-value: shows how likely it is that the observed difference between exposed and non-exposed days could have occurred by chance.
    Smaller p-values indicate stronger evidence of a real association.

    ● < 0.01: Strong evidence — unlikely to be due to chance
    ● 0.01 – 0.05: Moderate evidence — possible real effect
    ● > 0.05: Weak evidence — difference may be random

The p-value is calculated using a statistical test that compares the number of symptom days on exposed versus non-exposed days. It does not measure how large or important the effect is — only how confident we are that the difference is real.

The most useful results combine a meaningful risk increase with a low p-value. A low p-value with a very small risk change, or a large risk change with weak statistical evidence, should both be interpreted cautiously.

Traffic Light Summary

● Strong / reliable
● Moderate / interpret cautiously
● Weak / limited confidence

Note: Variability (±) in metrics indicates model performance across different subsets of your data. More data and consistent logging improve reliability.

Understanding Confidence Intervals

Many metrics, like ROC AUC and Symptom Recall, are reported with a ± value (e.g., 0.78 ± 0.04). This represents a confidence interval, which shows how much the metric varies across different subsets of your data.

  • Narrow intervals (small ± value): The model’s performance is consistent — results are more reliable.
  • Wide intervals (large ± value): The model’s performance varies more depending on which data is used — interpret with caution.
  • In your plots, the error bars on allergen odds ratios show a similar concept: longer bars indicate greater uncertainty in how strongly a particular allergen is associated with symptoms.

In practice, confidence intervals help you understand not just the average performance, but also the certainty of that performance. Metrics with green traffic-light colors and narrow intervals are the most trustworthy, while metrics with orange/red colors or wide intervals should be interpreted cautiously.
