Model Metrics Guide

Understanding Model Performance

This guide explains how to interpret the performance of the various models used on this site. Each of the models (Exposure Ranking, Dose–Response, Risk ) have specific metrics that indicate how well it is performing and how much confidence to place in its results. Their details are described below, along with a traffic-light color system to help you quickly assess the reliability of the results. Note that this guide is a tool for guidance, not a medical diagnosis.

Exposure Ranking Model

The Exposure Ranking model analyses your logged exposure and symptom data to highlight potential patterns. A logistic regression model is used to examine the relationship between logged exposures and symptom occurrence. This model returns an odds ratio, which indicates how much more likely symptoms are when exposed to the exposure. The metrics below help you understand how well the model is performing and how much confidence to place in its results.

  • ROC AUC: measures the model's ability to distinguish likely from unlikely exposures.
    ● ≥ 0.75: Good – strong separation
    ● 0.65 – 0.74: Moderate – some useful signal
    ● < 0.65: Weak – limited reliability

  • Symptom Recall: the proportion of symptom-linked exposures the model correctly identifies.
    ● ≥ 0.70: Good – most triggers captured
    ● 0.55 – 0.69: Moderate – some triggers may be missed
    ● < 0.55: Low – use results with caution

  • Sample Size: the number of logged exposure–symptom events used by the model.
    ● ≥ 200: Good – reliable patterns
    ● 75 – 199: Moderate – patterns emerging
    ● < 75: Low – preliminary, results may be unstable

  • Exposure Co-occurrence: some exposures are often logged together. High co-occurrence may indicate that multiple exposures are consumed simultaneously, making it harder to isolate individual effects.
    ● no co-occurrence: Good – reliable odds ratios
    ● co-occurrence present: Caution when interpreting odds ratios

Dose–Response Model

The odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval is estimated using an Ordinal Logisitc Regression Model, which determines how the probability of being in a higher symptom intensity category changes with exposure volume.

OR > 1: Higher exposure volume is associated with greater odds of more intense symptoms.
OR = 1: Exposure volume has no association with symptom intensity.
OR < 1: Higher allergen volume is associated with lower odds of more intense symptoms (protective effect or inverse relationship).

The 95% confidence interval (CI) indicates the precision of the estimate: If the CI does not include 1, the association is considered statistically significant. If the CI includes 1, the association may be due to chance.

    ● Confidence interval minimum is > 1
    ● Confidence interval maximum is < 1
    ● Confidence interval includes 1 but is mostly higher or lower
    ● Confidence includes 1

Risk & Statistical Evidence

These metrics describe how symptom risk differs between days with and without exposure to a specific exposure, and how confident we are that the difference is not due to chance.

The p-value is calculated using the Fisher exact statistical test which compares the number of symptom days on exposed versus non-exposed days. This test is commonly used when comparing 2 categorical variables. It does not measure how large or important the effect is only how confident we are that the difference is real.

The most useful results combine a meaningful risk increase with a low p-value. A low p-value with a very small risk change, or a large risk change with weak statistical evidence, should both be interpreted cautiously.

  • A positive value means symptoms were more common on exposure days.
    A negative value means symptoms were less common on exposure days.

    ● ≥ +10%: Meaningful increase in symptom risk
    ● +3% to +9%: Small to moderate increase
    ● < +3%: Little or no difference

  • p-value: shows how likely it is that the observed difference between exposed and non-exposed days could have occurred by chance.
    Smaller p-values indicate stronger evidence of a real association.

    ● < 0.01: Strong evidence — unlikely to be due to chance
    ● 0.01 – 0.05: Moderate evidence — possible real effect
    ● > 0.05: Weak evidence — difference may be random

Traffic Light Summary

● Strong / reliable
● Moderate / interpret cautiously
● Weak / limited confidence

Understanding Confidence Intervals

Many metrics, like ROC AUC and Symptom Recall, are reported with a ± value (e.g., 0.78 ± 0.04). This represents a confidence interval, which shows how much the metric varies across different subsets of your data.

  • Narrow intervals (small ± value): The model’s performance is consistent — results are more reliable.
  • Wide intervals (large ± value): The model’s performance varies more depending on which data is used — interpret with caution.
  • In your plots, the error bars on exposure odds ratios show a similar concept: longer bars indicate greater uncertainty in how strongly a particular exposure is associated with symptoms.

In practice, confidence intervals help you understand not just the average performance, but also the certainty of that performance. Metrics with green traffic-light colors and narrow intervals are the most trustworthy, while metrics with orange/red colors or wide intervals should be interpreted cautiously.
